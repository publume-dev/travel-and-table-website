Earthwork dining is a trend of curating experiential, artistic culinary moments designed around dining in and with nature, exemplified by events like Charles Michel's food painting picnic. This month, The Ever-Changing Table dinner series unfolds as part of Art Biesenthal's culinary program, where leaves replace porcelain and vegetables become sculptural centrepieces.

The trend manifests in specific events that shape participant experiences: Banquet on the Sea, an aquatic dinner by culinary artist Alix Lacloche, took place in Marseille's waters with a table built from old fishmonger's cold room panels and a menu featuring locally foraged seaweed soup; Farm Is Table, held last fall at Treiber Farms in Peconic, New York, featured a table and seating carved into the dirt, with local oysters served off repurposed irrigation pipes, at $125 per ticket. These events offer diners direct engagement with nature and local ingredients.

Earthwork dining is not entirely new: Bread & Puppet Theater has served house-made sourdough rye and pumpernickel loaves in Vermont's fields since the 1960s, and Outstanding in the Field, a traveling outdoor dinner series by land artist Jim Denevan, started in 1999 and continues today, serving hundreds of diners at communal tables worldwide.

The claim that earthwork dining is 'increasingly popular' lacks supporting data and is based on anecdotal examples; its growth is not quantified.
